# Payroll budgeting for a business

Payroll is often one of the largest and most consistent expense categories for
any business with employees, making it a critical part of financial planning.

## What payroll budgeting includes

- **Gross wages/salaries**: the base pay owed to employees before deductions.
- **Employer-side statutory contributions**: employer contributions to
  provident fund (EPF), employee state insurance (ESI), and other mandated
  benefits, which add to the total cost of employing someone beyond their
  quoted salary.
- **Benefits**: health insurance, bonuses, paid leave accrual — costs the
  business bears beyond direct salary.
- **Payroll processing costs**: software, payroll service fees, or accounting
  staff time dedicated to running payroll.

## Fully-loaded cost of an employee

A common mistake is budgeting only for an employee's quoted salary, without
accounting for the additional statutory and benefit costs layered on top —
the actual cost to the business ("fully-loaded cost") is often noticeably
higher than the salary figure alone. Underestimating this gap can lead to
under-budgeting for hiring plans.

## Payroll as a fixed vs semi-variable cost

Salaried employee payroll is generally a fixed cost (doesn't change month to
month regardless of sales volume), while commission-based or hourly staff pay
is more variable, scaling with sales activity or hours worked. This distinction
matters for break-even analysis (see break_even_analysis.md and
fixed_vs_variable_costs.md).

## Planning for payroll growth

When budgeting for hiring, it helps to project the fully-loaded cost of new
hires against expected revenue growth they'll support — hiring ahead of
revenue can strain cash flow, while hiring too far behind revenue growth can
limit the business's ability to serve demand. This is general education on
payroll budgeting concepts — specific statutory contribution rates and
compliance requirements vary by jurisdiction and should be confirmed with a
payroll specialist or accountant.
