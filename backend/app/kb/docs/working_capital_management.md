# Working capital management

Working capital is the difference between a business's current assets (cash,
inventory, receivables — things convertible to cash within a year) and current
liabilities (accounts payable, short-term debt — obligations due within a year).

Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities

## Why working capital matters

Positive working capital means a business has enough short-term resources to
cover its short-term obligations — a basic indicator of short-term financial
health. Persistently negative working capital can signal cash flow trouble,
even for a business that is profitable overall.

## The working capital cycle

This describes the time it takes to convert resources into cash:

1. Cash is spent on inventory/raw materials.
2. Inventory is converted into finished goods and sold (often on credit).
3. Cash is collected from customers (accounts receivable).

The longer this cycle, the more working capital a business needs to sustain
operations without running short on cash — a business with a 60-day cycle needs
significantly more buffer than one with a 15-day cycle.

## Ways businesses manage working capital needs

- **Negotiating supplier terms**: extending how long the business has to pay
  suppliers, effectively shortening the net cash conversion cycle.
- **Speeding up collections**: reducing how long it takes to collect from
  customers (see invoicing and receivables practices).
- **Inventory management**: holding less excess inventory reduces cash tied up
  in stock sitting unsold.
- **Short-term financing**: using a line of credit or working capital loan to
  bridge gaps during the cycle, rather than letting operations stall.

## A simple health check

Comparing the working capital cycle length against how much cash reserve or
credit line the business has available gives an early sense of whether the
business can comfortably fund its operations through a full cycle, or whether
it's at risk of a cash crunch during normal operations.
