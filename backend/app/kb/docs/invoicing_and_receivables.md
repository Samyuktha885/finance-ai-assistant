# Invoicing and accounts receivable management

Accounts receivable is money owed to a business by its customers for goods or
services already delivered but not yet paid for. Managing this well is central
to healthy cash flow.

## Key invoicing practices

- **Clear payment terms**: stating due dates explicitly (e.g. "Net 30" means
  payment due 30 days from invoice date) avoids ambiguity that leads to delays.
- **Invoicing promptly**: sending invoices immediately after delivering goods or
  services, rather than batching them, shortens the overall payment cycle.
- **Itemized detail**: invoices that clearly break down what's being charged for
  reduce disputes and payment delays caused by confusion.

## Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)

DSO measures the average number of days it takes a business to collect payment
after a sale. A lower DSO means faster cash collection. It's calculated as:

DSO = (Accounts Receivable / Total Credit Sales) × Number of Days in Period

A rising DSO over time can be an early warning sign of collection problems or
customers stretching out payments, even if total sales are growing.

## Common ways to reduce collection delays

- Offering small discounts for early payment (e.g. "2% discount if paid within
  10 days").
- Setting up automated payment reminders before and after the due date.
- Requiring partial upfront payment (deposit) for large orders or new clients.
- Following up promptly on overdue invoices rather than letting them age
  silently — the longer an invoice goes unpaid, the less likely it is to be
  collected in full.

## Aging receivables

Categorizing outstanding invoices by how overdue they are (e.g. current, 30
days overdue, 60 days overdue, 90+ days overdue) helps prioritize collection
efforts on the invoices most at risk of becoming uncollectable, rather than
treating all receivables the same.

This is general education on invoicing practices — specific payment terms and
collection policies should be tailored to the business's industry and customer
relationships.
